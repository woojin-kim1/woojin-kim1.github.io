Job Market Paper

Consumer Learning and Competitive Inefficiency

We study price competition in a differentiated-product duopoly in which a consumer observes prices before deciding whether to acquire costly information about which product better matches her preferences. Observed prices determine the value of learning, while the consumer's learning decision shapes how firms set prices. We characterize the unique mixed-strategy pricing equilibrium in which both firms are active. In equilibrium, sufficiently large price differences override product-fit information in the consumer's product choice, and hence the equilibrium fails to achieve ex post efficiency even when information is free---a distortion absent under monopoly. Higher information costs reduce the product differentiation created by consumer learning, intensify price competition, and raise consumer surplus. Total surplus is maximized either with free information or with no learning, depending on whether the allocation gain from favoring the advantaged firm outweighs the matching gain from learning. At sufficiently high information costs, the equilibrium enters a quasi-monopoly regime in which the consumer does not learn and purchases from the advantaged firm, and further increases in information costs raise the market price and lower consumer surplus. Finally, we endogenize the information friction and show that the firm may provide more product-fit transparency than is socially optimal.

Working Papers

Entry and Optimal Screening: Quantity Caps and Endogenous Market Segmentation

with Afshin Nikzad and Guofu Tan

We study how entry reshapes monopoly screening under asymmetric information. In our model, an incumbent with full contractual flexibility moves first, followed by an entrant restricted to linear pricing. The firms compete to serve a downstream buyer with privately known willingness to pay and inelastic demand. Although the incumbent can offer an arbitrarily complex menu, its optimal mechanism consists of a single quantity-payment option. We establish this result in two steps. Starting from an arbitrary menu, we first construct a class of alternative menus that preserve the entrant’s best response and use an extreme-point argument to show that the incumbent can restrict attention to menus with at most two options. We then reduce any such two-option menu to a single option by reallocating quantity from lower- to higher-valuation types. The incumbent’s optimal quantity-payment option can be implemented through a quantity cap and a per-unit price below the entrant’s. The cap allows the incumbent to target intermediate-valuation types without competing directly for high-valuation types, who instead purchase from the entrant, thereby endogenously segmenting the market. Relative to the monopoly benchmark, entry reduces the incumbent’s profit while increasing both consumer surplus and total surplus.

Predicting Merger Price Effects (Draft available upon request)

A central concern in merger review is whether a proposed merger will raise prices and reduce consumer welfare. This paper examines how accurately the CPT-UPP method, proposed by Farrell and Shapiro (2010b), predicts merger price effects under logit demand. By combining upward pricing pressure with cost pass-through, CPT-UPP provides quantitative predictions using pre-merger information. We study mergers in which all firms combine into a single firm and fully characterize the prediction bias in symmetric equilibrium. When two firms merge, CPT-UPP overestimates the price effects below a unique threshold for each firm's pre-merger market share and underestimates them above it. When more than two firms merge, it consistently underestimates the price effects. We also conduct simulations for asymmetric duopolies to examine how the prediction bias varies with market-share asymmetry. These findings highlight the importance of market structure when interpreting CPT-UPP predictions in merger reviews.
